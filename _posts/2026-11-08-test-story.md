The highway entered the mountains just after sunrise, carrying us from a familiar town into a landscape that seemed to rearrange itself around every bend. When the wind shifted, the sound of a bell reached us from somewhere below, thin and intermittent in the enormous space between the slopes. The scene was beautiful, certainly, but what stayed with me was the complicated relationship between the scenery and the people living inside it. What had felt like a series of separate encounters now belonged to a single journey, connected less by the highway than by the time spent moving along it.

There were schoolyards, fuel stations, half-finished houses, and laundry hanging from balconies, all the familiar evidence that a postcard is somebody else's home. A brief conversation about directions became a conversation about family, work, weather, and the curious reasons visitors come to see familiar places. An old stone building stood beside a modern shop, and neither appeared troubled by the other's presence in the same narrow street. From the window I watched fields giving way to stone walls, stands of trees, and the pale ribbon of a river far below us. A shopkeeper swept dust from his doorway while delivery vans negotiated the narrow road, each driver behaving as though the entire street belonged to him.

Travel often begins with the illusion that a place can be understood by moving through it, but the closer we came, the less certain I became. At every bend there was another view of the mountains, sometimes open and generous, sometimes half concealed by clouds moving across the ridgeline. Late in the day we reached a ridge overlooking the route behind us, and the road appeared impossibly small against the mountains it crossed. It was the kind of road on which distance means very little, because each kilometer introduces another reason to slow down or stop altogether.

From there the geography made more sense, with streams converging beneath the road and settlements occupying the small pockets of usable land. I found a room above a quiet street, opened the window, and listened to voices drifting upward from the restaurants closing for the night. I tried to imagine the same journey before the tunnels and bridges, when the passage between these valleys depended on weather, animals, and daylight. Beyond the village, the highway tightened against the hillside, and a low wall separated the pavement from a drop that made the passengers quiet. I had brought a notebook, intending to record the small observations that make a journey memorable, but much of the day resisted tidy sentences.

A weathered photograph hung above the counter, showing the same village decades earlier, recognizable only by the church tower and mountains behind it. Nothing in the landscape seemed hurried, although the evidence of change was visible in new roofs, repaired bridges, and advertisements along the road. I had expected the landscape to be the central story, but it was the people encountered along the way who kept interrupting that expectation. By then the light had changed, turning the upper slopes bronze while the buildings in the valley disappeared into a cool blue shadow. Outside, sunlight reached the far side of the valley before touching the street, leaving the town suspended in a long interval between night and day.

A roadside sign promised a viewpoint, so we followed a gravel track upward until the houses below looked like a scattering of stones. In the morning there would be more roads, more villages, and another opportunity to discover how little a map can say about a place. The buildings grew fewer, and for a while the journey consisted only of the engine, the changing light, and the next bend beyond the windshield. The road kept turning, and with each turn the country looked a little different from the picture I had carried in my head before arriving.

The proprietor pointed toward a narrow road climbing above the settlement and explained a route that sounded straightforward until he began naming its turns. The driver slowed for a herd of animals crossing the pavement, and for several minutes nobody seemed especially interested in moving anywhere quickly. A small collection of houses appeared below the road, their roofs pressed together as though the village had settled there to escape the wind. The mountains gave the place an apparent permanence, yet every village revealed evidence of movement, departure, return, and the ordinary adjustments of life. The road rose gradually through the hills, leaving the ordinary sounds of the town behind as the afternoon light moved across the valley.

One of them asked where I was going, then offered three different suggestions before agreeing that the original plan was probably good enough. By the time the first lights appeared in the next town, the mountains were almost invisible, present mainly as a dark boundary against the remaining sky. For now, the notebook contained a few names, several unfinished observations, and the impression that this journey would take longer to understand than to complete. We returned to the highway as evening approached, passing a bus stop where two travelers stood with bags at their feet and no obvious concern about time.

At the edge of town, an old bridge carried us over the water and into another stretch of countryside marked by fields and scattered farms. Near the square, a few people gathered outside a bakery and watched the passing cars with the patient curiosity of people who already knew the place. The valley opened suddenly, presenting an arrangement of rooftops, orchards, and distant peaks that seemed too carefully composed to be accidental. The answer was never quite the same twice, which seemed appropriate for a road that had already offered us so many versions of the same valley.

The following morning began with bread, tea, and an argument among the people at the neighboring table about which road was actually the shortest. By late afternoon the road was wet in places, reflecting the sky in long narrow patches that flashed beneath the tires as we passed. We stopped beside a roadside café where the coffee was strong, the tables uneven, and conversation carried farther than the traffic outside.
