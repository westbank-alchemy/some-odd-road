From the ridge, the road looked like a thin line scratched into the side of the mountain. It appeared to go nowhere, disappearing behind a series of folds before returning on a distant slope. Our driver told me that the town was only a few hours away, provided the weather held. Below us, a cluster of rooftops caught the morning sun while the valley remained in shadow.

We had started in a market town where buses, trucks, and motorbikes converged around a small plaza. A woman sold fruit from baskets arranged on a blanket. Across the street, two mechanics were working beneath a vehicle propped on blocks of wood. The smell of bread drifted from an open doorway, and someone had turned up a radio loud enough to reach the opposite corner. I bought water at a kiosk and waited while the driver spoke to three people who each seemed to have a different view about the road ahead.

The first stretch passed fields divided by low stone walls. Families worked in small plots while animals grazed on the steeper ground. Every so often the driver slowed to let a loaded truck negotiate a bend. The road was paved in some places, bare earth in others, and held together everywhere by the determination of people who needed it to remain passable. After a while we stopped counting the rough sections. They were not exceptions to the journey; they were the journey.

At a roadside stand we ate soup from deep bowls while a television played in the corner. The owner had set plastic chairs outside for customers who preferred the view. From there, I could see the road descending toward a bridge and climbing out again on the far side. A dog slept beneath a chair until a motorbike approached, at which point it woke, barked once, and returned to sleep. The owner described the next valley in practical terms: where the road turned, where we might find fuel, and which bend became slippery after rain.

Travelers tend to ask what a place is like, as though an answer could be separated from the effort of reaching it. The conversation at the stand suggested a different set of questions. Could a truck make it through after a storm? How long would a sick person spend reaching the nearest clinic? What happened to the market when the bridge closed? The mountains were impressive, but their beauty could not explain the schedules, costs, and inconveniences of living among them. The people at the table seemed more interested in those matters than in the view.

A landslide had recently covered part of the route. The road crew had cleared enough earth for one lane and left a pile of rock against the shoulder. We waited while another vehicle came through, then drove slowly across the repaired section. Fresh tracks in the dirt were the only sign that the work had been completed. The driver did not celebrate the crossing. He asked me to keep an eye on the opposite side for traffic, and I spent the next several minutes watching the bends with the concentration of someone suddenly given a small responsibility.

Near the top of the pass, the engine began to sound different. The driver opened his window and listened, then pulled over beside a concrete marker. He lifted the hood and studied a hose with the calm suspicion of a man who had been disappointed by machines before. I stood outside in the cold while two trucks climbed past us. Their drivers leaned on their horns before taking the bend, the sound traveling across the valley and returning faintly from the far slope.

The problem appeared minor. The driver tightened something, closed the hood, and told me we could continue. Still, I noticed that he kept one hand near the temperature gauge for the next half hour. The mountains had not changed, but my attention had. I was looking less at the peaks and more at the engine, the road surface, and the distance between us and the next settlement. I had briefly understood a feature of travel that does not fit easily into a photograph: its dependence on people who know what to do when an ordinary machine begins to fail.

At a high village we stopped for tea. A narrow schoolyard sat behind a wall painted with bright letters. Children were leaving through the gate in small groups, some walking downhill and others waiting for rides. A man carrying a coil of electrical cable spoke with our driver, pointing toward the sky. Clouds were collecting above the ridge. From the doorway of a shop I watched a woman tie down a sheet of plastic over bags of grain. She worked quickly, as if the weather had made a promise that she expected it to keep.

The rain arrived before we left. It began with a few large drops and became a steady drumming on the roof. The road darkened, and shallow streams of water followed the wheel tracks toward the ditch. We drove with the windows closed and the heater on. The driver took each bend carefully, leaving room for vehicles that might appear around the curve. I tried to match the landscape outside to my map, but the roads on the screen were clean colored lines. They did not include loose gravel, unexpected puddles, or the decisions being made in the driver's seat.

As we descended, the clouds lifted enough to reveal the next valley. The town we had been heading toward appeared as a band of rooftops along the river, with a bridge crossing to the main square. I felt relief, followed almost immediately by disappointment that the trip was nearly over. The feeling was irrational. We had been on the road all day, and I was tired of sitting still. Yet the slow progress had created a temporary world in which every turn was interesting because nobody could say with certainty what lay beyond it.

We entered the town just as shops were closing. Motorbikes passed beneath strings of lights. People queued outside a bakery, and a football game was underway on a hard court beside the road. A child chased a ball into a puddle and carried it back dripping to the others. The driver dropped me near a small guesthouse, helped unload the bag, and refused my attempt to make too much of the journey. He had driven the route before and would drive it again. By tomorrow afternoon, today's difficult sections would already have become part of his routine.

I climbed to the guesthouse roof before dinner. From there the road was almost invisible, concealed by the buildings and slopes that had defined the day. The mountains looked peaceful at a distance. The bridge, the trucks, the damaged shoulder, and the roadside stand disappeared into the broad arrangement of light and shadow. I took one photograph, then put away my phone. What I wanted to remember was not merely the valley as it looked from above, but the hours spent moving through it, depending on small acts of judgment by people whose names I might never know.
