The square was almost empty when I first crossed it. A street sweeper pushed a cart past a row of benches, and a delivery van stood with its rear doors open beside a café. The buildings around the pavement belonged to different periods and disagreed about almost everything: scale, decoration, color, and the proper relationship between a window and a wall. Morning sunlight reached the upper floors long before it reached the ground. I had come to look at a church on the far side, but the square kept offering reasons to stop before I got there.

At one corner, a bookseller was arranging used volumes on a table. Their covers ranged from faded cloth to glossy photographs. He lined them up according to a system known only to himself, then moved several back when he noticed they weren't straight. A woman stopped to inspect a cookbook, flipped through a few pages, and returned it to the stack. They exchanged a few words. She left without buying anything, and the seller resumed his work as though the conversation had been an expected part of the morning's inventory.

The church doors were open, but a small sign inside discouraged photographs. I put away my phone and stepped into the cool interior. A woman at a desk sorted candles by size. Two visitors stood beneath a painted wall looking upward without speaking. The light was dim enough that the colors took time to separate from the shadows. I stayed near the back, watching people enter, make small gestures of respect, and leave. The room seemed less concerned with explaining itself to strangers than with maintaining a rhythm older than any particular visitor.

Outside, the bells began to ring. Their sound crossed the square and echoed between façades in uneven waves. A group of schoolchildren hurried past their teacher, scattering pigeons that had been collecting crumbs beneath the benches. A cyclist slowed, waited for an opening, and continued between a bus stop and the corner of a flower bed. The square had become busy without ever becoming crowded. I moved to a café terrace where a waiter placed a menu on the table, then removed it when I ordered coffee before opening it.

From the terrace I could see the church, an administrative building, and a cluster of storefronts selling things no traveler would normally photograph. One window displayed keys and locks. Another advertised phone repairs. A shop with a handwritten sign sold cleaning supplies. These were the unremarkable businesses that held the square together between ceremonies, demonstrations, and postcard visits. I wondered what a resident would choose as the square's defining feature. It might be the church, but it might just as easily be the pharmacy that stayed open late.

The architectural history of a city is often presented as a sequence of clean replacements: one government falls, a monument is erected, a style gives way to the next. On the ground, the changes are untidier. A restored façade adjoins a patched storefront. A plaque explains a vanished building while a new advertisement covers part of the wall beside it. A tram passes a statue whose original audience could never have imagined the vehicle. The city does not replace one chapter with another. It stacks them, sometimes badly, and asks the inhabitants to make sense of the result.

I crossed to the opposite side and found a pedestrian passage leading behind the main buildings. The noise of the square faded immediately. There were waste bins, parked cars, a cat sleeping on a warm hood, and kitchen windows open above a narrow courtyard. A cook stood outside smoking while a colleague carried crates through a back door. I watched them for a moment, embarrassed by how much more convincing the city seemed from this service entrance. The grand square was intended to be looked at. The passage existed because people had work to do.

By lunchtime, the benches were occupied. Office workers ate sandwiches, tourists consulted maps, and an older couple shared a paper bag of pastries. The bookseller's table had attracted a few customers. The cyclist returned in the opposite direction. The waiter who had served me moved chairs into the shade, anticipating the path of the afternoon sun. Everywhere, small calculations adjusted the public space to practical needs. Nobody asked permission from the buildings. Their impressive histories remained in place while the day unfolded beneath them.

A brief rain began without warning, pushing people beneath awnings. I took shelter near the bookseller, who covered his table with a plastic sheet in a single practiced motion. He offered me a corner of the dry space. We stood together while drops struck the awning and ran into the gutter. He told me where to find another bookshop farther uphill, drawing a little map on the back of a receipt. When the rain stopped he returned to his books, and I carried the map across the wet pavement as carefully as if it were something valuable.

The second shop was closed. A paper notice on its door offered hours that did not correspond to the time I was standing there. I looked back down the street toward the square and decided not to investigate. The failed errand was fitting. Cities are rarely arranged around the interests of the people visiting them for a few days. I walked instead along a side street where apartment balconies held potted plants, umbrellas, and folded laundry. A woman opened a window above me and shook out a small cloth, sending a cloud of dust into the light.

When I returned toward evening, the square had changed again. The tables were full, children ran between the benches, and the bookseller had packed up his stock. The church remained on the far side, still the most visually arresting building in the scene. But I could not see it without remembering the key shop, the newspaper argument, the rain, and the map drawn on a receipt. The square had offered its official image to me in the morning. Over the course of the day it had become a place where other people conducted their lives.

I crossed it once more on my way to dinner. Someone had left a paper cup beside the bench where the older couple had sat. A worker collected it and put it into a bag, then continued toward the next litter bin. The church bells sounded faintly as I reached the opposite curb. I did not turn around for a final photograph. I had one already, taken from the café. The better record was the imperfect list of things that had happened while I was there, none important enough to make the guidebook and all sufficient to fill the day.
