The departure board said nineteen thirty, but the clerk shook his head when I pointed to it. The bus would leave later, he explained, and perhaps from the platform on the other side of the building. He wrote a number on the back of my ticket. Then he crossed it out and wrote another. Outside, wind carried dust across the station lot in pale sheets. Several passengers had already arranged their bags beside a concrete pillar, where the wall offered a little shelter. I joined them because it seemed more reliable than the information on the board.

The station was at the edge of a small town in southern Argentina, with a petrol station across the road and low houses beyond it. There was nothing dramatic about the setting. A truck idled beside a tire shop. A teenager wheeled a bicycle with one flat tire down the sidewalk. In the waiting room, a television showed a football match without sound. Every few minutes someone glanced up at the screen and then returned to checking the doors for signs of our driver.

I had bought the ticket because the road north looked simple on a map. The line crossed broad open country, passed a handful of settlements, and ended in a city where I intended to stay two nights. It seemed straightforward enough to require no particular planning. That confidence began to fade when the clerk informed me there would be no food stop for several hours. The kiosk sold sandwiches in triangular plastic boxes and a few cakes wrapped in cellophane. I purchased both, along with enough water to make my bag awkward to carry.

At eight, a woman arrived with two children and a suitcase tied shut with a belt. She had missed the earlier bus, she told the people beside me, and needed to reach her sister before morning. One child fell asleep with his head on her shoulder. The other inspected the ticket machine as if considering how it might be dismantled. A man wearing a work jacket helped lift the suitcase onto a bench. Everyone had a reason to be somewhere else, but for the moment we had become a temporary household organized around a delayed departure.

The bus appeared shortly after dark. It came around the corner slowly, its headlights rising and dipping over the uneven pavement. The driver stood in the open doorway while passengers crowded toward him. He checked each ticket with deliberate care, despite the wind and the line of people waiting. Our bags vanished into the compartment beneath the vehicle. The children climbed aboard first. I found a seat next to a window streaked with dust and discovered that its curtain was held in place with a knot.

We left town past rows of orange streetlights. For a while there were workshops, fences, and signs advertising rooms. Then the last lit building disappeared and the darkness became absolute. The road was visible only where our headlights touched it. I could occasionally make out a wire fence or a white post marking a curve. The landscape that had seemed immense in daylight had become a narrow strip of asphalt and a black pane of glass that returned my own reflection whenever I tried to look outside.

An hour later, the driver stopped beside a building with a single fluorescent light above its door. Nobody announced a break, but half the passengers stood up at once. Inside, the counter held a thermos, packaged biscuits, and a tray of empanadas that had been waiting longer than we had. A woman sold hot water for the thermoses passengers carried. I watched the small economy unfold: coins exchanged, paper cups filled, food offered across the counter, and everyone checking the bus every few minutes to make sure it was still there.

Back aboard, I sat beside a man who worked on road crews. He was traveling home after several weeks away. His hands were broad and heavily marked, and he spoke with the economy of someone who expected to be interrupted by the engine. He asked where I was headed, then described the next town by reference to a bridge that might or might not be open. I had not thought to ask about bridges. He shrugged at my concern. There was always another route, he said, although it could take much longer.

Most of the passengers slept. A phone lit up across the aisle, briefly illuminating a woman's face before she folded it into her coat. The child in the row ahead murmured something in his sleep. Through the windshield I could see the driver maintaining a steady, unhurried line along the road. His work depended on attention to things the passengers could not see: the shape of a bend, the strength of a crosswind, the place where an animal might enter the road. My own exhaustion was a luxury made possible by his concentration.

At the next stop the woman with the children woke them gently. The boy who had studied the ticket machine now carried a stuffed animal by one ear. Their suitcase emerged from the storage compartment, belt still fastened around its middle. A man waiting near the platform waved when he saw them. I watched the family reunite beneath a buzzing light, then disappear through a gate. The bus pulled away before I could see which house they entered. For the first time all evening, our temporary household had lost some of its members.

I arrived the following morning with stiff legs and a half-eaten sandwich. The city was already awake. A cleaner moved a long broom across the terminal floor, scattering a line of dust toward an open door. Taxis waited with their engines running. I stood for a moment beside the luggage compartment, remembering how uncertain the evening had felt when I boarded. The journey had been ordinary transportation, hardly an adventure by local standards. Yet most of its interesting details were precisely the things a map could not show.
