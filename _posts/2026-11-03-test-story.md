The first bell came from somewhere below the road. It rang once, then again, its sound rising through a fog that had covered the valley since dawn. From the passenger seat, I could see only a few yards of gravel and the dark outline of trees. Our driver leaned toward the windshield as if that would help. On the dashboard, a tiny plastic icon rocked back and forth with every pothole. We had been climbing for an hour, although climbing suggested more progress than the road was prepared to acknowledge.

In the last village we had stopped for bread and coffee. The bakery had no sign, only a window fogged from within and a small queue of people who appeared to know each other. A boy carried six loaves in a cloth bag almost as large as he was. An older woman studied us while counting coins into the baker's palm. When our driver asked about the road ahead, everybody offered an opinion. From the doorway I understood only their gestures: a finger pointing uphill, a hand tilted to imitate a landslide, and a shrug that could have meant anything.

There are places where the landscape arrives before the settlement, announcing itself on postcards and road signs for miles. Here it happened in reverse. A garage appeared beside the road, followed by a cluster of fences and two houses built against a steep slope. Only when the clouds shifted did I realize that a line of immense mountains had been standing behind them all along. The peaks were not a destination so much as an interruption in the ordinary business of getting through the day. A man in rubber boots crossed the road holding a blue bucket and did not look up.

We stopped beside a shop that sold batteries, biscuits, and a surprising variety of hair dye. Its owner had arranged the merchandise into careful columns, with candles beneath the register and packets of tea stacked beside a portable radio. The radio was quiet. She told our driver that the telephone signal had disappeared after last night's storm. He held up his own phone to confirm it, then put it away. I bought a chocolate bar I didn't want because it seemed impolite to stand there taking in the room without buying something.

Beyond the shop stood a tower, narrow and severe, rising above the roofs as if it belonged to another system of measurement. Another appeared around the next bend. Their stone walls looked simultaneously permanent and vulnerable, the kind of buildings that could survive a century but not necessarily a badly planned road repair. I tried to imagine the valley before electric wires, trucks, and tourists carrying cameras. The exercise did not get very far. Every time I looked closely, the present asserted itself through a satellite dish or a bright plastic chair resting against ancient stone.

At lunch we sat beneath a covered porch while the fog moved through the street. A dog slept under the neighboring table. Two men argued over something on a mobile phone, passed it between them, and finally turned the screen toward the waitress for a third opinion. She considered the matter for less than a second before waving them away. A plate of potatoes arrived with herbs scattered across the top. I had been thinking about the mountains, but the strongest impression of that hour was the warm bowl in my hands and the dog opening one eye whenever a chair scraped the floor.

I went walking after the meal, following a lane that narrowed between walls and then broadened unexpectedly into a yard. A woman was gathering washing from a line. She pointed me toward the main path without waiting for a question, perhaps having watched enough visitors make the same mistake. The route passed a small cemetery and a gate with two different locks. Someone had repaired the gate with fresh wood but left its old iron hinges in place. I stopped there longer than the view seemed to justify, interested by the quiet practicality of a repair that wasn't trying to look new.

The driver found me as evening approached. He had obtained news of the road from a passing truck, although it was unclear whether the news was good. We loaded the bags, checked the tires, and drove through the same streets we had walked earlier. The shop was closing. The bakery window was dark. In one courtyard a man was chopping wood with the patient rhythm of someone who knew exactly how much remained to be done. I had spent the day searching for a view and could remember every ordinary object we passed more clearly than the mountains themselves.

As we left, the fog descended again. I heard a bell, perhaps the same one from the morning, but I could not see where it was hanging. It sounded once and then stopped. The driver turned on the headlights, and the road shortened into a pale corridor between the trees. Somewhere behind us the valley continued with its errands, arguments, dinners, and small repairs. I had been permitted to look at it for a day. The people who lived there would have to get up tomorrow and do the whole thing again.
